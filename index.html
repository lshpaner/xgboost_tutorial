What XGBoost is

XGBoost, short for extreme gradient boosting, fits an ensemble of decision trees one at a time. Each new tree is trained on the errors the ensemble has made so far, so the combined prediction improves round by round.1 The idea of boosting as gradient descent in function space is older,2 and much of what the library adds is engineering: a regularized objective, a second-order approximation, and a split search built for large tables.

Core idea

Rather than fitting one large model, train many small ones and add them up. Each tree only needs to be slightly better than nothing, because its job is to correct the residual error left by everything before it.

The boosting loop

1.Initialize with a constant
2.Compute gradients and Hessians
3.Grow a tree on them
4.Add it, scaled by \(\eta\)
5.Repeat until done

Step 1.Initialize the prediction

Start from a single constant: the mean for regression, the log-odds for classification.

$$F_0(x) = \arg\min_{c} \sum_{i=1}^{n} L(y_i, c)$$

Everything that follows is a correction to this one number.\(c\) is the same value for every sample. For squared error the minimizer is the mean of \(y\); for log loss it is the log-odds of the positive class.

Step 2.Compute gradients and Hessians

Differentiate the loss with respect to the current prediction, once and then twice:

$$g_i = \frac{\partial L(y_i, \hat{y}_i)}{\partial \hat{y}_i} \qquad h_i = \frac{\partial^2 L(y_i, \hat{y}_i)}{\partial \hat{y}_i^2}$$

Step 3.Grow a new tree

Choose each split by the reduction in the regularized objective it buys:

$$\text{Gain} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L + \lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R + \lambda} - \frac{(G_L + G_R)^2}{H_L + H_R + \lambda}\right] - \gamma$$

\(G\) and \(H\) are sums of gradients and Hessians over the samples that land in the left (\(L\)) and right (\(R\)) children.The \(-\gamma\) at the end is what makes a split cost something. A candidate whose gain does not clear it is pruned, which is why a large \(\gamma\) yields shallow trees even at a high max_depth. A split is kept only when the gain is positive.

Step 4.Update the prediction

Add the new tree, shrunk by the learning rate:

$$F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \eta \, f_m(x)$$

Step 5.Repeat

Go back to step 2 with the updated prediction. Stop at the tree budget, or earlier if a validation score stops improving.

What sets it apart

Regularization in the objective

The loss carries an explicit complexity term, so the tree-growing step is penalized for extra leaves and large weights:

$$\text{Obj} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} L(y_i, \hat{y}_i) + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \Omega(f_k), \qquad \Omega(f) = \gamma T + \frac{1}{2}\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{T} w_j^2 + \alpha \sum_{j=1}^{T} |w_j|$$

\(T\) is the number of leaves and \(w_j\) the weight of leaf \(j\).

Pruning after growth

Trees grow to max_depth and are then pruned from the bottom up, removing any split whose gain falls below \(\gamma\).

Missing values

Each split learns a default direction for missing values from the data, so no imputation is required beforehand.

Parallel split search

Features are pre-sorted into blocks so candidate splits can be evaluated across threads, even though the trees themselves are still built sequentially.

Column subsampling

Each tree (or level, or node) can see a random subset of features, borrowed from random forests as a cheap way to decorrelate the ensemble.Row subsampling comes from stochastic gradient boosting,3 where fitting each tree on a random half of the data turned out to regularize as well as it sped things up.

The math behind the splits

Second-order Taylor expansion

At round \(t\), the loss is expanded around the previous prediction and truncated after the quadratic term:

$$L\!\left(y_i, \hat{y}_i^{(t)}\right) \approx L\!\left(y_i, \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)}\right) + g_i f_t(x_i) + \frac{1}{2}h_i f_t^2(x_i)$$

The approximation is quadratic in \(f_t\), so for any fixed tree structure the best leaf weights can be solved in closed form rather than by iteration.Closed form here means a formula you evaluate once, with no loop. Set the derivative of a parabola to zero and solve: that is all equation (7) is doing.

Optimal leaf weight

Setting the derivative to zero for leaf \(j\), with \(I_j\) the samples that fall in it:

$$w_j^* = -\frac{\sum_{i \in I_j} g_i}{\sum_{i \in I_j} h_i + \lambda}$$

Substituting \(w_j^*\) back into the objective gives the per-leaf score that the gain formula in section 2 compares across candidate splits.

A workable order

Fix a modest learning rate (around 0.1) and let early stopping on a validation set choose the number of trees. Then tune depth and min_child_weight together, then the subsampling fractions, and only last the L1 and L2 penalties. Lower the learning rate and re-fit once the rest is settled.

Against other ensembles

Three ways of combining trees that are easy to confuse.Classic GBM means the Friedman formulation as implemented in, for example, scikit-learn's GradientBoostingRegressor: first-order gradients and no objective-level regularization.

When to reach for it

A good fit
  • Tabular data. Rows and columns with mixed numeric and categorical features are where boosted trees still lead.
  • Thousands to millions of rows. Enough signal for sequential correction to pay off.
  • Class imbalance. scale_pos_weight and custom objectives are built in.
  • Explanations needed. Gain-based importance and SHAP values come cheaply.
  • Production pipelines. Mature, well-supported, and fast at inference.
Look elsewhere when
  • The inputs are images, audio, or free text; deep learning handles those.
  • The relationship is mostly linear; a regularized linear model is simpler to explain and maintain.
  • The sample is tiny (under a hundred rows); boosting will memorize it.
  • Latency per prediction must be minimal; hundreds of trees add up.
